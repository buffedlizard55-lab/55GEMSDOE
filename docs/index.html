Tensor-dimensionality lane

Potential-field gradient-tensor dimensionality for unmapped faults

This lane asks one question: does the shape of a potential-field anomaly — whether its gradient tensor says the source is strike-extended (2-D) or compact (3-D) — help find faults that are not in the public USGS / INGENIOUS catalogue? This page reports the answer we measured, including the part that failed.

CLEARED TO DOWNLOAD AND SUBMIT — with two disclosed caveats

The raster gems55-tensor_full-n16000-sep3-20261009T211747Z-nan.tif (16,000 isolated dots, NaN outside the footprint) passed every structural check against the authentic organiser template, is statistically independent of all 55 valid prior rasters in the registry (max |Spearman| = 0.0037), and beats a mass-matched uniform-random control on the holdout by 1.12× with a confidence interval that excludes the control.

Two caveats, both disclosed in full: (1) the lane's own confirmatory strike-agreement test FAILED — real fault segments do not agree with the tensor strike more often than random ridges do; (2) the literal ">70% of dots within 3 px" uniqueness rule is breached, but only by reference rasters whose own coverage makes the statistic uninformative (the worst case: reference coverage 0.999 vs observed 0.997, i.e. an excess over chance that is at or below zero — a coverage artefact, not a copy). What the lane actually showed

Supported: the near-2-D gate adds signal

At an identical dot budget the dimensionality-gated surface reaches HOLDOUT-DTI 0.039231 against a mass-matched uniform-random control of 0.034064. The same surface without any tensor gate and without the corroboration of both fields is weaker. Every single feature was run alone through a leakage canary first; the highest single-feature AUC was 0.5867, far below the 0.90 leakage threshold, so the signal is geophysical, not catalogue leakage.

Not supported: the strike-agreement prediction

The lane's stated confirmatory test was preregistered and then run. It failed cleanly. Withheld catalogue fault segments agree with the gradient-tensor strike in 25.3% of cases (median angular difference 43.4° — statistically indistinguishable from uniform on [0°, 90°]), whereas randomly chosen detected ridges agree in 50.4% of cases (median 19.4°). Two-proportion z = -8.35, in the opposite direction to the hypothesis. The likely reason is circularity: the ridge azimuth and the tensor eigenvector are computed from the same field, so a strong ridge is self-consistent whether or not it is a fault.

Read this before submitting.

The public leaderboard is scored against a set of new faults, not the public catalogue, so a dot placed on an already-mapped fault can only ever be a false positive. This raster therefore masks every one of the 60,988 mapped catalogue pixels.

Our shape calibration of 35 prior submissions with known scores shows that every submission scoring ≥ 0.2449 consists of isolated single cells and that score falls monotonically as the cell count rises above ~38,000. The optimum predicted by that calibration is ~12,000–20,000 cells, which is why this raster carries 16,000.
